The Story

The Joe Montana Story

Every 49ers fan has a Joe Montana story, and most of them start the same way. Somebody's dad, somebody's uncle, a living room somewhere between Daly City and Danville, the clock under two minutes, and nobody in the house worried. That was the strange gift of Joe. You'd watch a game you had no business winning and feel calm, because he looked calm, and he was usually right to.

He was born June 11, 1956, in New Eagle, Pennsylvania, and grew up in Monongahela, a coal town south of Pittsburgh. At Ringgold High he played football, basketball and baseball, and he was good enough at hoops that North Carolina State offered him a basketball scholarship. He went to Notre Dame instead, partly because his boyhood idol Terry Hanratty had gone there.

South Bend made him a legend before the NFL had any idea. He kept coming off the bench to save games: a 21 to 14 win at North Carolina after coming in down eight, a 31 to 30 win over Air Force after trailing 30 to 10. In 1977 he went from third string to starter and the Irish won the national title. Then came the Chicken Soup Game in the 1979 Cotton Bowl, where he sat in the locker room fighting hypothermia, came back out down 34 to 12 late in the third quarter, and won it 35 to 34 as time expired.

None of that impressed the scouts much. One combine graded his arm a six out of nine. Three quarterbacks went ahead of him in the 1979 draft, all in the first round, and the 49ers took him at the end of the third, 82nd overall. Bill Walsh, in his first year running the team, saw something the grading sheets couldn't measure, and he brought him along slowly behind Steve DeBerg. Montana threw 23 passes as a rookie.

By late 1980 he was the starter, and on December 7 at Candlestick he showed us what was coming. The winless Saints led 35 to 7 at halftime. The 49ers tied it in regulation and won 38 to 35 in overtime, the first fourth quarter comeback of his pro career. A year later the 49ers went 13 and 3, and on January 10, 1982, he rolled right with three Cowboys chasing him and threw the ball Dwight Clark caught. Two weeks later he was Super Bowl MVP in Pontiac, Michigan, and San Francisco had its first championship.

Then came the run. The 1984 team went 15 and 1, the first team ever to win 15 regular season games, and in January 1985 he beat Dan Marino at Stanford Stadium, the first Super Bowl ever played in the Bay Area, with a record 331 passing yards. He wasn't perfect. The 49ers lost their first playoff game three straight years after that, and in the 1987 loss to Minnesota, Walsh pulled him early in the third quarter. In 1986 he hurt a disc in his lower back in Week 1, had surgery, heard doctors suggest he retire, and was back on the field about two months later.

The 1988 season nearly came apart. Steve Young had arrived in 1987, the trade rumors got loud, and the team sat at 6 and 5. Montana took the job back, the 49ers won the division, and they went through Minnesota and a 17 degree Soldier Field to Miami. Down 16 to 13 with 3:10 left and the ball at his own 8, he drove them 92 yards and hit John Taylor with 34 seconds to go. People remember the John Candy line in the huddle. I remember being sure he'd score.

His 1989 season is the best a 49ers quarterback has ever had, and I'll argue that with anybody. He completed 70.2 percent, posted a 112.4 passer rating that was then the best single season mark in league history, won the NFL MVP, and threw 11 touchdowns and zero interceptions in three playoff games, finishing with a 55 to 10 demolition of Denver and his third Super Bowl MVP. He won MVP again in 1990 and Sports Illustrated named him Sportsman of the Year. The 49ers were one win from a shot at three straight titles when Leonard Marshall hit him from the blind side in the NFC Championship Game.

That hit changed everything. A broken hand, then an elbow injury in the 1991 preseason, and he missed all of 1991 and nearly all of 1992 while Young became a star. He came back for the second half of the final regular season game in December 1992, on a Monday night against Detroit, and played well. It was his last game as a 49er. The quarterback controversy split the locker room and the whole Bay Area, he asked for a trade, and on April 20, 1993, he went to Kansas City.

With the Chiefs, wearing 19, he won two playoff games with comebacks and took Kansas City to the AFC Championship Game in his first year. In 1994 he beat the 49ers and Young 24 to 17 in Week 2, which was a weird afternoon to be a Niners fan, and he outdueled John Elway on a Monday night. He retired on April 18, 1995, at Justin Herman Plaza in San Francisco, with Walsh as emcee. Of course it was in San Francisco.

The 49ers retired number 16 on December 15, 1997, and he went into the Pro Football Hall of Fame in 2000. He finished with 40,551 yards and 273 touchdown passes, went 4 and 0 in Super Bowls with 11 touchdowns and no interceptions, and was named to the NFL's 100th Anniversary All Time Team. He turned 70 in June 2026.

These days he's a venture investor. He cofounded Liquid 2 Ventures in 2015 to back early stage tech companies, and in a February 2026 interview he credited Walsh with showing him how to put a good team together. Funny how that works. The guy who never panicked in a two minute drill ended up betting on founders for a living.

02
Under Pressure

Why He Was Clutch

You can measure most quarterbacks. With Montana it was the fourth quarter, and the numbers still hold up. Four Super Bowls, four wins, 122 passes, 11 touchdowns and not one interception. A 127.8 passer rating on the biggest stage the sport has. When he retired he owned the career playoff records for attempts, completions, yards, touchdown passes and wins.

The two drives everybody knows are the two that mattered most. In the 1981 NFC title game he started at his own 11 with 4:54 left, down 27 to 21 to the Cowboys, and went 89 yards. Seven years later in Miami he started at his own 8 with 3:10 left, down 16 to 13, and went 92. Same guy, same pulse, same result.

He wasn't automatic, and I'm not going to pretend he was. He threw three picks in The Catch game. He had three straight one and done playoff exits from 1985 to 1987, and the Vikings ran him off the field in the last one. He got knocked out of the 1990 NFC Championship Game with the 49ers up 13 to 9 and watched them lose 15 to 13. But when it was close and late, he's the one I'd hand it to before anybody who ever lived. I know that's the homer talking. I'm fine with it.

Walsh built the system, Rice and Clark and Taylor caught the balls, Lott and the defense held the line. Montana made it look like nothing. That's what clutch looked like in this town for a decade: a skinny kid from western Pennsylvania glancing at the clock like he had all day.

“Joe Montana is the greatest quarterback today, maybe the greatest quarterback of all time.”

Bill Walsh, 49ers head coach
after Super Bowl XIX, January 1985
03
The Big Moments

Clutch Moments

  1. December 7, 1980

    Down 35 to 7, and nobody quit

    The winless Saints came into Candlestick and led 35 to 7 at halftime. New Orleans still led 35 to 21 at the start of the fourth quarter. Montana brought the 49ers all the way back to tie it in regulation, and Ray Wersching won it with a field goal in overtime, 38 to 35. It was the first fourth quarter comeback of his NFL career, in his first year as the starter.

  2. January 10, 1982

    The Catch

    NFC Championship Game at Candlestick, 49ers down 27 to 21 to Dallas. The 49ers got the ball at their own 11 with 4:54 left. Montana moved them to the Dallas 6, and on third and three with 58 seconds left he rolled right with Ed Too Tall Jones, Larry Bethea and D.D. Lewis chasing him and threw high to the back of the end zone. Dwight Clark went up and got it. Wersching's extra point made it 28 to 27, the end of a 14 play, 89 yard drive. Montana had thrown three interceptions that day and it didn't matter one bit.

  3. January 24, 1982

    First ring, first MVP

    Super Bowl XVI at the Pontiac Silverdome against Cincinnati. Montana scored the game's first touchdown on a 1 yard run, hit Earl Cooper for a 10 yard score to finish a then record 92 yard drive, and the 49ers went in up 20 to 0 at the half. The defense made its famous goal line stand in the third quarter and the 49ers held on, 26 to 21. Montana went 14 of 22 for 157 yards with a touchdown passing and another rushing, and was named MVP.

  4. January 20, 1985

    Outdueling Marino at Stanford

    Super Bowl XIX at Stanford Stadium, the first Super Bowl ever played in the Bay Area. Everybody wanted to talk about Dan Marino. Miami led 10 to 7 after one quarter, and then the 49ers scored three touchdowns in the second. Montana went 24 of 35 for a Super Bowl record 331 yards and three touchdowns, ran five times for 59 yards and a score, and won his second Super Bowl MVP in a 38 to 16 rout.

  5. January 22, 1989

    92 yards in Miami

    Super Bowl XXIII at Joe Robbie Stadium, and the 49ers trailed Cincinnati 16 to 13 with 3:10 left, pinned at their own 8 after a penalty on the kickoff return. Montana took them 92 yards in 11 plays and threw a 10 yard touchdown to John Taylor with 34 seconds left for a 20 to 16 win. Harris Barton later told the story of Montana pointing out John Candy in the stands before the drive. He threw for a Super Bowl record 357 yards that night.

  6. January 28, 1990

    Five touchdowns, third MVP

    Super Bowl XXIV in the Superdome against John Elway and Denver. It was 27 to 3 at the half and 55 to 10 at the end, the most lopsided Super Bowl ever played. Montana went 22 of 29 for 297 yards and a then record five touchdown passes, three of them to Jerry Rice, and became the first player to win Super Bowl MVP three times. He finished that postseason with 11 touchdowns, no interceptions and a 146.4 passer rating.

  7. January 20, 1991

    The hit that ended an era

    NFC Championship Game at Candlestick, 49ers chasing a third straight title. Montana's 61 yard touchdown to John Taylor in the third quarter put them up 13 to 6, and it was 13 to 9 in the fourth when he rolled right on third down, stepped away from Lawrence Taylor and got hit from the blind side by Leonard Marshall. Broken hand, cracked ribs, bruised sternum. He was done for the day. Roger Craig fumbled with 2:36 left, Matt Bahr kicked his fifth field goal on the final play, and the Giants won 15 to 13. Montana never started another playoff game for the 49ers.

In Their Words

What They Said About Montana

“At his best, when Joe was in sync, he had an intuitive, instinctive nature rarely equaled by any athlete in any sport.”

Bill Walsh, 49ers head coach
looking back on Montana's career

“There have been, and will be, much better arms and legs and much better bodies on quarterbacks in the NFL, but if you have to win a game or score a touchdown or win a championship, the only guy to get is Joe Montana.”

Randy Cross, 49ers guard and teammate
looking back on Montana's career

“Joe was a great player and probably will go down as the best quarterback in the history of pro football.”

Dennis Green, Minnesota Vikings head coach and former 49ers assistant
December 1994, before the Vikings played the 49ers

“He goes, 'Check it out ... Look down there in the other end zone. You see him?… There's John Candy down there in the end zone.'”

Harris Barton, 49ers offensive tackle
recalling the huddle before the winning drive in Super Bowl XXIII

“Joe was born to be a quarterback.”

Jeff Petrucci, his high school quarterbacks coach
on Montana's early years
