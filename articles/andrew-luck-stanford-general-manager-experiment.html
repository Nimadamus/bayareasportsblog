Andrew Luck Is Running a Football Program, and the Rest of College Football Should Be Watching

A retired quarterback with a general manager title at his alma mater is either a vanity appointment or the first sane answer anyone has given to what college football turned into.

Bay Area Sports Blog Staff · Stanford

When Stanford gave Andrew Luck a general manager title, the easy read was that a rich school had found a job for a beloved alum. The harder read, and the correct one, is that Stanford looked at what college football had become and decided it needed the one thing the sport had never bothered to build: somebody whose actual job is the roster.

Think about what a head coach is now asked to do. Recruit high schoolers. Work the transfer portal in two windows. Manage name, image and likeness money like a capologist. Retain the players he already has against everyone else's money. Hire and keep a staff. And, somewhere in there, coach a football team on Saturday. Every other serious sport in the world separated those jobs decades ago.

That is the experiment. Luck runs the programme, the head coach coaches. In year two, the defining move has been made: Tavita Pritchard got the job, a first-time head coach who played quarterback here, backed Luck up, and then went and spent three years in the NFL learning how a real front office operates. That is not a sentimental hire. That is a hire that only makes sense if you believe the person picking it has thought hard about what the coach is actually for.

It might not work. Stanford's admissions standards are not moving, the money in this sport is not slowing down, and no organisational chart wins you a game against Notre Dame. If the Davis Warren era produces four wins and nobody comes to the games, all of this reads as a very smart plan that lost anyway.

It is worth being precise about why the structure helps rather than just sounding modern. A head coach evaluating a transfer is evaluating a player he will have to coach, which biases him toward players who fit what he already runs. A general manager evaluating the same player is asking a different question: what does this roster need in eighteen months, and what is this player worth against everyone else available at that price. Those are different jobs with different time horizons, and asking one person to hold both in his head during a season is how programmes end up with expensive rosters that do not fit together.

The second advantage is continuity. Coaches get fired. If the roster function lives with the coach, a firing resets everything, recruiting relationships, portal strategy, the money structure, all of it. If it lives with a general manager, a coaching change is a coaching change rather than a demolition. For a programme that has already been through several resets, that alone might be worth the experiment.

The scepticism is fair too. Luck has never done this before. Being a great quarterback tells you nothing about whether someone can value a left tackle against a slot receiver under a budget. And there is a version of this where the title is mostly a fundraising device and the real decisions get made the way they always were. Two years in, the Pritchard hire is the first piece of hard evidence either way.

But if it does work, if separating the roster job from the coaching job turns a programme that had genuinely bottomed out back into something, then a lot of athletic directors are going to find themselves explaining why they still have one exhausted man doing five jobs. That is why this is worth watching from Berkeley, from Eugene, from anywhere. The rest of our coverage is on the Stanford hub.
