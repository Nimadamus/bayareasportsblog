The Big Game: How Cal and Stanford Built the Oldest Rivalry on the West Coast

It started in 1892 with a future president as the student manager, it produced the most famous ending in the sport, and the trophy has been stolen more times than anyone at either school will admit.

Bay Area Sports Blog Staff · Bay Area

Before the Apple Cup, before the Civil War up in Oregon, before USC and UCLA existed as a thing anyone argued about, there was the Big Game. Cal and Stanford first played on 19 March 1892. Stanford won 14-10. It is the oldest rivalry on this coast and it has been running, with a few interruptions, ever since.

The origin story is genuinely absurd. The push for that first meeting came from Stanford's student manager, a young man named Herbert Hoover, who later found other work, and his counterpart at Cal, Herbert Lang. Two student managers named Herbert invented the defining sporting event of Northern California, and one of them ended up in the White House.

The rivalry's defining moment came on 20 November 1982, in the 85th meeting. Cal led, then Stanford's quarterback, a kid named John Elway, drove them down for a field goal and what everyone assumed was the win. Four seconds left. What happened next is called, simply, The Play: five laterals on the kickoff return, Cal players pitching the ball backwards to anyone still standing, and the return man crossing the goal line through the Stanford band, which had already come onto the field to celebrate. Cal 25, Stanford 20. Elway never played in another Big Game. People at both schools have been arguing about the legality of the third lateral for more than forty years and neither side is going to concede.

What they play for is the Stanford Axe, which has its own criminal history. Since 1933 it has gone to the winner of the Big Game, and it sits in the winning school's trophy case for a year with the scores of past meetings mounted beside it.

The overall ledger favours the farm. Through the 2025 meeting, the schools had played 127 times, with Stanford leading the series 65-51-11. That is a lot of November afternoons, a lot of stolen trophies, and a lot of people who will not let it go.

What keeps it alive is that it was never really about football rankings. These are two universities eleven miles apart across a bay, one public and one private, one that takes forty thousand undergraduates and one that takes a fraction of that, both of them convinced the other is insufferable. The football game is just where that argument gets settled once a year in front of witnesses.

That is also why the Big Game refuses to shrink when the teams are bad. There have been long stretches where neither side was going anywhere and the last Saturday still filled the stadium, because a losing season can be redeemed entirely by one afternoon. Ask anyone who was in Berkeley in 1982 what that team's record was. Nobody remembers. Everybody remembers the band.

It has survived a great deal: two world wars, an era when the schools stopped speaking about eligibility rules, decades of conference reshuffling, and most recently a realignment that dragged both programmes into a conference on the other side of the country. The rivalry outlasted the Pac-12 itself. It will outlast whatever comes after the ACC too.

In 2026 the Big Game lands on 21 November, in Berkeley, with new head coaches on both sidelines - Tosh Lupoi for Cal, Tavita Pritchard for Stanford - which is the sort of coincidence this rivalry produces roughly every generation. Our Cal and Stanford hubs carry the rest.
