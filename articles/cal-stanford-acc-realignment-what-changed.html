Cal and Stanford Play in the ACC Now, and Nobody Around Here Has Made Peace With It

Two Bay Area schools in a conference built around the Atlantic coast. The travel is absurd, the kickoff times are worse, and the rivalry that survived it is the one nobody planned.

Bay Area Sports Blog Staff · Bay Area

The Pac-12 died and the two Bay Area schools ended up in the Atlantic Coast Conference, which is a sentence that should still stop you cold. Berkeley is not on the Atlantic coast. Palo Alto is not on the Atlantic coast. Geography lost an argument with television money and this is what was left.

Look at what it means in practice. Cal's 2026 schedule spends 2026 travelling to Syracuse, Southern Methodist in Dallas, NC State in Raleigh and Virginia in Charlottesville. Stanford draws Duke, Georgia Tech, Wake Forest, Louisville and Virginia Tech. Those are not road trips, those are deployments. A student athlete in Berkeley with a Tuesday morning lab is crossing three time zones on a Thursday.

The second cost is the one nobody put in the press release: the kickoff windows. A conference whose television inventory is built around the eastern seaboard does not schedule around a Bay Area breakfast. Games that used to be Saturday afternoons in front of a full student section are now morning kicks played into a half-empty stadium, because the audience that matters is four hours ahead.

And the third cost is the rivalries. The annual dates against Oregon, against Washington, against the Los Angeles schools, the ones that gave a Cal or Stanford season its rhythm, are gone or reduced to occasional novelties. What replaced them are opponents most Bay Area fans could not place on a map, playing for standings that mean nothing locally.

Here is the part that is almost funny. The one fixture that survived all of it is the Big Game, which nobody had to protect, because both schools moved together. The rivalry that predates every conference either school has ever belonged to outlived the conference that was supposed to house it forever. It will still be played in November, in front of people who care, while the rest of the schedule happens at nine in the morning against a team from North Carolina.

The defence of the move is that it was survival, and that defence is not stupid. When the Pac-12 collapsed, the alternative for these two schools was a conference nobody had heard of or an independent schedule nobody would televise. The ACC offered relevance, even at a reduced financial share and an absurd travel bill. Given the options actually on the table, taking it was rational. That does not make it good. It makes it the least bad outcome of a process that never should have reached that point.

What gets lost in the accounting is the thing that made college football matter regionally in the first place: familiarity. A rivalry is built by playing somebody every year for fifty years until the grudges are inherited. You cannot manufacture that with a rotating ACC opponent who visits Berkeley once a decade. The Bay Area is not going to develop a blood feud with Wake Forest, no matter how many times the schedule pairs them.

There is a generational cost too. A kid growing up in the East Bay used to be able to watch Cal play Oregon and Washington and the Los Angeles schools, learn the map of their own conference, and care about it. That kid now watches a 9am kickoff against a school three thousand miles away, and the honest question is whether they bother to keep watching at all.

None of this gets undone. The money is spent and the contracts are signed. The most anyone around here can do is keep showing up for the games that still mean something, which is exactly what the Cal and Stanford sections are for.
