Oracle Park and McCovey Cove: Why the Ballpark Plays the Way It Does

A short right field on the water, a cavernous right-centre, a wind that eats fly balls, and a body of water named after a man who never got to hit into it.

Bay Area Sports Blog Staff · Giants

Every ballpark has a personality and most of them are marketing. Oracle Park's is real, measurable, and it has shaped how the Giants have been built for a quarter of a century.

The dimensions. The park opened in 2000. Left field is 339 feet, centre is 391, and right field is 309, which sounds like a bandbox until you understand what sits behind that right-field wall and how quickly the fence runs away from the plate into right-centre. The short porch is a target for maybe a dozen swings a season. Everything else that goes that way dies in the gap.

The wind. The genuine equaliser, and the reason batted-ball data at this park doesn't travel. Evening games get a cold, heavy marine air that takes real distance off a fly ball. Hitters who arrive here from warmer parks spend a season learning that the ball they crushed in July would have been out somewhere else.

What the numbers say. Recent park factors put runs at 94 and home runs at 78, against a league average of 100. Runs are suppressed slightly. Home runs are suppressed enormously, roughly a fifth fewer than a neutral park. Oracle Park isn't a mild pitcher's park. It's one of the hardest places in baseball to hit a ball out.

McCovey Cove. The water beyond right field is named for Willie McCovey, and the name came from sportswriters rather than a marketing department, the thought being that McCovey, who spent his career fighting the wind at Candlestick, would have put dozens of balls in there had he played on the water instead.

Splash hits. A home run that clears the right-field wall and reaches the water on the fly. The first belonged to Barry Bonds, off a Mets left-hander on 1 May 2000, which is about as fitting as sporting history gets. And here's the detail that tells you everything about the geometry: no right-handed hitter has ever put one in the Cove the opposite way. Not one, in a quarter of a century.

Why it matters to how the team is built. A park that eats home runs rewards contact, speed, defence and pitching, and punishes a roster built on slug. That isn't a coincidence in the even-year dynasty, those teams were pitching and defence and timely hitting, which is exactly what this park pays for. Every time the Giants have tried to build a lineup of sluggers, the park has quietly taken a percentage.

The other thing it is. The most beautiful place to watch baseball in the country, and it hasn't had enough October nights to match. Oracle Park Is Still Waiting is our column about that particular grievance. The current state of the roster is in our rebuild page, and the rest is on the Giants hub.
